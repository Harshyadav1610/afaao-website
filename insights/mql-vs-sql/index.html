MQL vs SQL: what the difference is and how to set the rules

By the Afaao team · November 23, 2026 · 6 min read

Few things cause more arguments between sales and marketing than the word MQL. Marketing says it sent plenty of leads. Sales says none of them were real. Usually both are right, because nobody wrote down what an MQL or an SQL actually means.

Part of our RevOps consulting guides.

Short answer

An MQL (marketing qualified lead) is someone marketing believes is worth sales attention, based on fit and engagement. An SQL (sales qualified lead) is someone sales has reviewed and agreed is a real opportunity to pursue. The handoff works when both teams agree on written rules for each and how fast sales follows up.

What an MQL is

A marketing qualified lead is a person marketing believes is ready for a sales conversation. Good MQL rules combine two things: fit (are they the kind of company and person you sell to) and intent (have they done something that shows interest, like requesting a demo or visiting pricing several times).

What an SQL is

A sales qualified lead is an MQL that sales has reviewed and accepted as a real opportunity. It usually means a conversation happened and basic qualification is confirmed, such as need, timing and the right person. Many teams add an SAL (sales accepted lead) step in between, meaning sales has looked at it and agreed to work it.

The difference in one line

Marketing decides who becomes an MQL. Sales decides who becomes an SQL. The rules for both are agreed together.

Example rules for a small B2B team

  • MQL: job title in your buyer list, company in your target size and region, and one high intent action such as a demo request, or a lead score above an agreed threshold.
  • Not an MQL: students, competitors, personal emails with no company, or anyone outside your service area.
  • SQL: a sales rep has spoken to them, confirmed a real need, a rough timeline and that they can influence the decision.
  • Recycled: not ready now. Goes back to marketing nurture with a reason, instead of disappearing.

How to build it in your CRM

  1. Write the rules on one page and get sales and marketing leaders to sign off.
  2. Map them to fields: lifecycle stage and lead status in HubSpot, or lead status and conversion in Salesforce. Our guide to HubSpot lifecycle stages shows how the stages fit together.
  3. Automate the MQL step with a workflow based on fit and intent, so nobody decides by hand.
  4. Route every MQL to an owner within minutes, with a fallback. See why HubSpot leads go unassigned if that part is breaking.
  5. Require a reason when sales rejects or recycles a lead, so marketing can improve the rules.
  6. Report MQL to SQL conversion by source every month and adjust the rules once a quarter.

Signs your definitions are broken

  • Sales ignores MQLs and works their own list instead.
  • Marketing hits its MQL target while pipeline stays flat.
  • Nobody can say how long it takes sales to follow up an MQL.
  • The same lead shows as an MQL in one report and not in another.

FAQ

Quick answers

What comes first, MQL or SQL?

MQL comes first. Marketing qualifies the lead, then sales reviews it and turns it into an SQL if it is a real opportunity.

What is a good MQL to SQL conversion rate?

It varies a lot by business, lead source and how strict your MQL rules are. Track your own rate by source and focus on improving it over time rather than chasing a benchmark.
